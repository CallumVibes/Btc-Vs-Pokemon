      The argument

      Both sides, put as well as they deserve.

      1. The supply is fixed and you can check it yourself. Twenty-one million, enforced by every node running the rules. Pokémon's scarcity is a corporate decision — The Pokémon Company owns the printers and has reprinted chase cards when demand got loud. Modern print runs already dropped prices 40–60%.
      2. Nobody's opinion sits between you and your holding. A PSA 10 is one private company's grade. Standards drift, re-slabs reprice, and pop reports come from the same firms that profit from grading. A bitcoin needs no adjudicator.
      3. You can leave with it in seconds. Sell a card and you're looking at days to weeks, plus 10–20% to eBay or an auction house. Sell bitcoin and it's near-instant at a few basis points. That gap is a permanent tax on the card side of every comparison.
      4. It's divisible and portable in a way an object never is. You can send £8 or £800,000 across a border from a phone. You cannot post half a Charizard, and the whole one needs insuring, storing and protecting from damp, fire and theft.
      5. Condition risk only runs one way. Cardboard degrades. A corner ding, a bit of sun, a bad reseal, and value evaporates. A UTXO doesn't develop whitening.
      6. Counterfeits are an unsolved problem there and a solved one here. Fakes get better every year and grading is the expensive patch. Every node validates every coin for free.
      1. Thirty years of demand that has nothing to do with speculation. People want these because they were eight once. That's a deeper foundation than most assets have, and it's shown no sign of exhausting — 2026 is the franchise's 30th anniversary.
      2. The drawdowns are gentler. High-grade vintage largely held through the post-2021 correction. Bitcoin routinely gives back 70–80% peak to trough. If you can't sit through that, the theoretical return is irrelevant.
      3. You cannot lose it to a forgotten passphrase. No seed, no phishing, no exchange collapse, no bricked hardware wallet. The failure modes are ones ordinary people already understand: fire, theft, water.
      4. It sits outside the financial system entirely. No rails to be cut off, no exchange to freeze, no regulatory attack surface. A binder in a cupboard is remarkably hard to sanction.
      5. Supply of the old stuff genuinely does shrink. Vintage cards get damaged, lost and thrown out every year. The 1999 print run only goes down, and high-grade survivors get rarer with time.
      6. You get to actually enjoy it. It's a physical object you can show people. That's not an investment argument, but it is a real return, and it's one bitcoin can't pay.

      Where they agree: neither pays a dividend, neither produces anything, and both are worth exactly what the next person will pay. Anyone holding one and mocking the other for that is standing on the same ground.

      What this is. Bitcoin against whichever Pokémon card is currently the most expensive on a shortlist of grails — Base Set holos, Neo Genesis Lugia, the alt-art VMAX chase cards. If a different card takes the crown, the page follows it. Card prices are Cardmarket sold data for raw, ungraded copies. Graded slabs trade far higher.

      Where the numbers come from. Bitcoin from CoinGecko, refreshed every five minutes while the page is open. Cards from the Pokémon TCG API, which carries Cardmarket's sold data — that only moves once a day, so polling it faster would return the same number. Card prices are quoted in euros; the EUR→GBP rate is derived from bitcoin's own price in both currencies — BTC/GBP ÷ BTC/EUR.

      Setting your own price. Tap the card's price to type what it actually goes for — a PSA 10, a 1st Edition, whatever your friends really own. It's saved on your device and feeds the per-bitcoin count, the sats figure and the live battle. It won't move the percentages or the chart: scaling a price by a constant leaves its rate of change identical, which is exactly why a guessed grade multiplier would have been decoration.

      The honest caveat. This is one card against one asset over whatever window you pick, which is a much narrower claim than "Pokémon beats Bitcoin." Change the range and the winner can flip. Not investment advice.
